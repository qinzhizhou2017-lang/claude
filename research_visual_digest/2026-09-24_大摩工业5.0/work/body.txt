Implications for a Multipolar World – “Made in China” Shifting to “Made by China”
Source: UN Comtrade, Growth Lab at Harvard University, Morgan Stanley Research. Note: based on latest available 
Product Complexity Rankings.
China has greater export capacity in high-complexity 
goods than all other major Asian economies combined
We estimate that only ~40% of China-to-US exports can be readily 
substituted: supplier depth, process know-how and capital-
equipment ecosystems are considerably harder to replicate than 
assembly lines
Source: Growth Lab at Harvard University, United Nations, UN Comtrade, Morgan Stanley Research.
• Tariffs, technology controls and China+1 strategies have reduced direct US–China trade, but the evidence points more to 
trade rerouting and selective final-assembly relocation than wholesale migration of productive capacity
• We see a reconfigured form of globalization: final production becomes more geographically dispersed, while Chinese 
capabilities remain embedded in industrial expansion overseas. In other words, it marks a shift from “made in China” toward 
a broader “made by China” model

Source: National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), Morgan Stanley Research

Source: National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), Morgan Stanley Research
US electrification (1880s-1920s)
• US electrification shows that a general-purpose technology can sustain elevated capex intensity for decades, not just years. 
It was not a one-off equipment replacement cycle – it created a new physical layer for the economy
• The physical build-out was significant. The capex cycle was not only about building power plants and grids; factories 
themselves had to be rewired around electric motors, new layouts and new production methods
Similarly, for China Industry 5.0 AI-native manufacturing, it also requires both an infrastructure layer and a factory-rebuild layer: 
compute, grid, storage, cooling, industrial software, robotics, sensors, edge AI and factory automation

Japan's heavy n (1960s -1975)
• Japan’s postwar heavy-n cycle shows that a capex supercycle can last far longer than a normal replacement 
cycle. Japan’s high-growth period ran from the mid-1950s to the early 1970s, with real GDP growth frequently exceeding 10%
• The cycle was driven by an “investment-inducing-investment” mechanism: an initial rise in investment created demand for 
machinery, steel, construction, electricity and other industrial inputs, which then created the need for another round of 
investment
The read-across to China Industry 5.0 is that if AI infrastructure, factory automation, industrial software, power equipment, 
robotics and physical AI adoption reinforce each other, China’s cycle can also become a multi-year, possibly decade-long 
industrial reinvestment regime
